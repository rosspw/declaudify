# Tells, by strength

A tell is one observable sign of slop: a name, a line you can quote, a fix. This catalog is ordered by how
rarely a careful writer would make the move on purpose. **Act on sight** tells justify an edit on one
sighting. **Act in clusters** tells need two or more in a passage. **Weak alone** tells count only with
company. The order matters more than the list: structure first, words last, because word habits change with
every model release and structural habits do not.

Leave a tell alone inside a quotation, a title, a proper noun, code, or a passage that discusses the phrase
rather than uses it. A tell that is clearly the writer's own voice, earning its place, stays.

Sources, merged: the owner's humanize-writing catalog, Wikipedia's "Signs of AI writing" (via
blader/humanizer's 26 patterns), stop-slop's structures, de-slop's tiers, and the Claudish dictionary
(`claudish.md`).

## A. Act on sight

Every act-on-sight tell has a **Keep** line: the nearest thing that looks like the tell and is not. Read it
before you cut. Over-correction is the most common complaint about every skill in this category.

**A1 · Not X but Y.** "It's not about speed, it's about trust." "Not just a feature, a shift." "This doesn't
mean X. It means Y." The reversed "Y rather than X." A clipped negative tail ("..., no guessing"). The
negative half names something no one claimed so the positive half sounds bigger. State Y. Keep the contrast
only when X is a belief the reader actually holds. → "Trust matters more than speed."
*Keep:* "The error happens on save, not on upload." Both halves are facts, and the reader needs the second.

**A2 · One-line closers and dramatic fragments.** A one-sentence paragraph restating the one before it.
"That's the real win." "That distinction matters." "Read that again." "Let that sink in." A sentence after an
example that names what it showed. A row of fragments ("No prior. No nostalgia."). "Every. Single. Day."
Cut the closer that repeats; merge fragments into a sentence with a claim. Keep one short sentence when it
carries a new fact.
*Keep:* "I disagree. The data isn't there yet." Short, and each sentence says something new.

**A3 · Staged run-up.** "Here's the thing." "Let's dive in." "Here's what you need to know." "Honestly?"
"Look," "Real talk." "Let me be clear." "The uncomfortable truth is." The writer announces the point or
stages candor instead of making the point. Delete the run-up and state the point. "Honestly" inside a casual
sentence is fine; the standalone opener is the tell.
*Keep:* "Honestly, I haven't decided yet." Candor about an actual state, not a drumroll.

**A4 · Sayings that sound deep.** "At its core." "The real question is." "What really matters." "X is the
language of Y." "X becomes a trap." "It's an execution problem dressed up as a leadership problem." An
ordinary point dressed as a hidden truth. Replace the saying with the specific claim.
*Keep:* a quoted or discussed aphorism, and a metaphor the writer chose that makes the point clearer.

**A5 · Arguing with no one.** "This isn't about X." "I'm not saying X, but." "To be clear." "Don't get me
wrong." "A tempting approach would be." "You might think... but." An objection or option nobody raised,
usually a leftover from a draft. Remove it; keep any real claim inside it. Keep an objection the text
attributes to someone and answers in full.
*Keep:* "This won't prevent outages, but it limits the data you lose in one." A real limit, stated as one.

**A6 · Faux-insight setup.** "The part most people miss." "What nobody tells you." "Here's what I find
interesting." "Plot twist:" "What if I told you." Flatters the writer as lone expert. Cut the setup; let the
claim stand.

**A7 · Chatbot residue.** "Great question!" "Certainly!" "I hope this helps." "Would you like me to..."
"Let me know if." "You're absolutely right." "You're right to push back." Template blanks like [Your Name].
Knowledge-limit disclaimers ("as of my last update"). Remove the wrapper; keep the content.
*Keep:* greetings, thanks and sign-offs in a real email or support reply; "as of March" when it dates the data.

**A8 · Status-report voice.** "Verified. Both pass. Fix is in." "Done. Tests green. Shipped." Clipped
fragments reporting state with no actor and no reader. Full sentences with a subject: "I ran both suites and
they pass."

**A9 · Writing about the document instead of its subject.** "The table below compares." "This section is
organized by owner." "Generated from the vendor's pricing pages." "This function was added to replace."
"Anything unconfirmed is flagged rather than guessed." The text describes itself or its own method. Describe
the subject. Keep a source credit the reader can follow; state a convention once only when the reader cannot
see it.

**A10 · Re-explaining what the reader has.** In a reply or a thread: restating the question, walking the
diagnosis, proving a plan will work, the decision in the last line. The reader has the context; each
sentence reads fine alone, so this survives sentence-level cleanup. Lead with the decision; keep only the
reasoning that would change whether the reader agrees. (Only when you can see the conversation or the
text is plainly a reply.)

**A11 · Weasel attribution and borrowed authority.** "Experts agree." "Studies show." "Industry reports
suggest." "Widely regarded as." A list of prestige outlets with no claim attached. Name the source and what
it said, or cut the claim, or put it in Gaps. Never invent a source.

**A12 · Inflated significance.** "Marks a pivotal moment." "Stands as a testament." "Plays a vital role."
"Underscores its importance." "Setting the stage for." "The future looks bright." "Despite these
challenges... continues to thrive." The fact underneath is usually fine. Keep the fact, drop the
significance, end on the last concrete fact.

**A13 · Fake-profound kicker.** A final line that turns the point into an aphorism, a cute metaphor or a
mic-drop ("That's the whole game."). Delete it. Do not rewrite it into a better metaphor. End on the clearest
concrete sentence already there, or a plain next action.

**A14 · Summary-recap ending.** "In conclusion." "Ultimately." "Overall." A last paragraph that restates the
piece. "Key takeaways" that repeat the body. End on the last real point, a takeaway that adds something, or
the next action.

**A15 · Engagement bait.** "Agree?" "Thoughts?" "What would you add?" "Repost if this resonates." "Drop a
comment." Remove. A real open question is fine.

## B. Act in clusters

**B1 · Forced triads.** "Innovation, inspiration, and insights." Three parallel examples, three short facts
then a lesson, three adjectives. Ideas arrive in threes to sound complete. Use the number the meaning has:
one, two, four. Keep three real items.

**B2 · Repeated sentence openings and robotic rhythm.** Several sentences starting with the same word;
every paragraph the same length; the same sentence shape in a row. Merge, vary, or change the subject.
Deliberate repetition for rhythm is the writer's and stays.

**B3 · Shallow -ing riders.** "..., highlighting the importance of." "..., underscoring." "..., reflecting
the team's commitment." "..., ensuring." An -ing phrase bolted onto a fact to make it sound deeper. Keep
the fact; replace the rider with the actual consequence or cut it.

**B4 · Avoiding is, are and has.** "Serves as." "Stands as." "Functions as." "Boasts." "Features." "Offers."
"Represents." "Refers to." Use is, are, has, or the real action. "The app serves as a centralized hub for
sponsor management" → "The app tracks sponsors, drafts and due dates in one place."

**B5 · Overused AI vocabulary.** delve, leverage, utilize, robust, seamless, holistic, dynamic, synergy,
cutting-edge, innovative, transformative, game-changing, paradigm shift, tapestry, realm, beacon, landscape
(figurative), ever-evolving, multifaceted, meticulous, intricate, paramount, comprehensive, nuanced (as
filler), journey (figurative), testament, foster, cultivate, empower, unlock, unleash, harness, elevate,
embark, navigate (figurative), showcase, underscore (verb), pivotal, crucial, vital, enduring, vibrant,
quietly, actionable, insights (as filler), at scale (no number), move the needle, low-hanging fruit,
thought leader, best practices (unless citing one), data-driven, customer-centric, in today's world, in the
age of, it's worth noting, it's important to note, at the end of the day, when it comes to, in terms of, in
order to, going forward, furthermore, moreover, additionally. One formal word is not a tell. Several, close
together, are. Plain word or nothing. For the agent dialect, see `claudish.md`.

**B6 · Reassurance with no evidence.** cleanly, coherent, robust, verified, comprehensive, thoroughly tested,
production-ready, battle-tested. Either name the check ("42 tests pass", "ran it on the three sample files")
or cut the word. If you cannot tell what was checked, cut and write a Gap.

**B7 · Hedge stacking and stacked qualifiers.** "It might perhaps be worth considering." "Could potentially
possibly." "To be fair, it's also possible." One hedge, and only when the uncertainty is real. Keep scope
statements, safety notices and honest corrections. Ordinary "perhaps" and "tends to" are human.

**B8 · Importance puffery and empty adverbs.** "Significantly improved." "Truly." "Genuinely." "Deeply."
"Fundamentally." "Incredibly." "Crucially." "Importantly." "Absolutely" as an opener. Cut when they add
nothing; keep when they carry real emphasis, contrast or the writer's spoken rhythm. Replace "significantly
improved" with the number if the text has it; if not, Gap.

**B9 · Bold as decoration and bold-label bullets.** Words bolded for no reason. A vertical list where every
item is "**Label:** sentence" and the labels carry nothing. Remove the bold. Turn a labeled list into prose
unless the labels are real reference content a skimmer needs. Never remove bold, ❌, ✗ or ⚠️ from a warning,
a prohibition or a danger sign: a reader who skims will read "**Pinch the nose**" as an instruction. If a
symbol goes, its meaning goes into words.

**B10 · Decorative headings and template structure.** Title Case headings. Emoji or arrows in headings or
list items. A horizontal rule between every section. A heading over a two-sentence section. A heading
repeated as the first sentence under it. A heading written for effect ("The decision, on one screen") instead
of for content ("How the six options compare"). Intro, three points, conclusion. Sentence case; one heading
per section a reader jumps to; headings that say the claim.

**B11 · Colon reveal and label-colon framework.** "The best part: it learns." "The answer is
straightforward:" Observations packaged as "Label: description" pairs to fake a framework. Write the plain
sentence. Colons are for lists, labels and quotes.

**B12 · Rhetorical setup.** "What if...?" "Think about it:" "Why? Because..." A self-answered question. A
triple rhetorical question hook. Make the point.

**B13 · False agency and narrator-from-a-distance.** "The complaint becomes a fix." "The decision emerged."
"The data tells us." "The culture shifts." "Nobody designed this." "People tend to." Things doing human
verbs; a lecturer floating above the scene. Name the person who did the thing. "You" when the reader is the
one in the seat.

**B14 · Synonym cycling.** "The agent reviews it. The assistant scores it. The tool suggests fixes." One
entity, three names. Pick the right word and repeat it.

**B15 · Vague connection.** "Associated with." "In connection with." "Linked to." "Tied to." Says two things
are connected without saying how. Name the relationship the text gives. If it does not say, keep the vague
wording rather than invent a role.

**B16 · Sales language outside sales copy.** "Nestled." "In the heart of." "Renowned." "Breathtaking."
"Stunning." "Must-visit." "Rich cultural heritage." "Diverse array." "Commitment to." State what the thing
is.

**B17 · Stacked abstract nouns.** "Creativity, passion, joy and drive." Three or more abstract nouns for
emotional weight. One concrete claim, or one noun.

**B18 · Generic or hyperbole opener.** "Communication is key in remote teams." "Everything changed." Two or
three credential lines before the point. Three stat fragments in a row. Open with the specific story,
number or claim already in the text. If there isn't one, Gap; don't invent one.

**B19 · Formatting slop.** Emoji in headings; bullets where two sentences of prose read better; headers over
tiny sections; arrow chains (A → B → C) standing in for sentences; exclamation points the content didn't
earn; ALL CAPS for emphasis ("HUNDREDS of customers": use the number); parenthetical pileups. Format follows
content and format (`format-map.md`).

## C. Weak alone

**C1 · Dashes.** Em and en dashes as the universal connector. Many good writers use them, so one is nothing;
a text full of them is a tell. Rule for the final text: none in short copy; one or two in a long piece when
they clearly beat a comma, colon, period or parentheses; match a writer's sample that uses them; zero if the
writer bans them. Dashes inside code, commands, paths and URLs are never touched.

**C2 · Passive voice and missing subjects.** "Mistakes were made." "No configuration file needed." Use the
active voice when it makes the actor clearer. Passive is fine when the actor is unknown or irrelevant.

**C3 · Hyphenated pairs after the noun.** "The report is high-quality." Keep the hyphen before a noun ("a
high-quality report"), drop it after. Dictionary compounds (third-party, cross-functional) keep it.

**C4 · A single transition word.** One "however", "additionally", "that said" is a human habit. A row of
them, or one starting every paragraph, is B2.

**C5 · Curly quotes.** Most editors auto-curl them. Match the target format only when the user or the file
makes it obvious.

**C6 · Reading complexity creep.** Clusters of three-syllable words and nested clauses in conversational
writing. Shorter words, shorter sentences. Does not apply where precision needs the terms.

**C7 · Wh- and "So" openers.** "What makes this hard is..." Paragraphs starting with "So". A crutch when
stacked. Lead with the subject or the verb. One is fine.

## D. Substance checks

Run these even when the prose is clean. Clean but hollow is still a problem and the user should hear it.

- **Portability.** Could this sentence, or the whole piece, move unchanged to another person, company or
  product? Then it lacks earned specifics.
- **Only this writer.** Is there firsthand experience, a named decision, a real number with context, a real
  example? If none, say so in Gaps.
- **Same point twice.** A second paragraph that restates the first without adding depth.
- **Recycled framing.** "The future of X is Y." Generic lessons. Fortune-cookie takeaways from a real story.
- **Disguised plug.** A product pitch dressed as a lesson learned.
- **The reader test.** After the first paragraph, can a reader say in one sentence what this is for and what
  it wants from them? If not, move 2 is not done.
